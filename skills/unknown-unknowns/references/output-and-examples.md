# Output shape and examples

## Output shape

1. **The gap that matters most.** One or two sentences: what the user may not know, and what it changes in their plan.
2. **Other gaps,** numbered, most important first. Each one:
   - what you may not know (plain words);
   - why it matters here (which part of the plan it changes);
   - evidence (the source, or the user's words it contrasts with);
   - cheapest next move (read this, ask this person, try this small test).
3. **Assumptions to confirm,** numbered, as either-or questions. Each has a recommended answer and one line on what it changes. The first question is the one whose answer can change the others.
4. **Terms you may need,** only if the field is new to the user. Each term with a one-line meaning, then the user's request restated the way a practitioner would say it.
5. **Already open.** One line listing the questions the user named themselves, so they are not lost.
6. **Not checked.** One line on what you could not verify or find.

Keep it short. Leave out any section that has nothing in it. The examples below are illustrations of the shape; their facts stand in for what the scout would return with sources.

## Examples

### First book, self-published

**Picture.** Goal: publish a finished novel. Plan: "upload it to Amazon next week, I'll do the cover myself in Canva". Known: the manuscript is edited. Constraints: little money. Open: "should I use a pen name?"

**Gap that matters most.** Most self-published books are found through the store's categories, keywords, and the cover as a small thumbnail, not through the book's page. "Upload next week" leaves no time for any of the three, and they are hard to fix once reviews and rankings start.

**Other gaps.**

1. Enrolling the ebook in the store's exclusive programme means it cannot be sold elsewhere for that period. Changes: whether to go wide or exclusive, which is the first decision, not a later one. Evidence: the platform's help pages. Next move: read the programme terms before uploading.
2. Authors routinely order a printed proof before release, and proofs often show margin and cover-bleed problems that the screen preview hid. Changes: the timeline, because a proof takes days to arrive. Next move: order one proof copy.
3. A free ISBN from the platform usually lists the platform as publisher and cannot be used elsewhere. Changes: whether to buy an ISBN, which matters only if going wide.

**Assumptions to confirm.**

1. Is this book (a) one-off, or (b) the first of several? Recommended: (b), since you mentioned a second draft. Changes: cover and pen-name choices should then work for a series.
2. Is success (a) readers and reviews, or (b) income? Recommended: (a). Changes: price and launch plan.

**Already open.** Pen name.

### "Make my video look nicer"

**Picture.** Goal: a travel video that looks better. Plan: "play with the filters". Known: basic cutting. Open: none.

**Gap that matters most.** Colorists split this into two jobs done in order: first correction (make shots accurate and match each other), then grading (give them a look). Filters jump straight to the look, which is why shots from different times of day still clash.

**Terms you may need.**

- Exposure: overall brightness.
- White balance: removing a colour cast so whites look white.
- Shot matching: making consecutive shots look like the same moment.
- LUT: a preset colour transform, applied after correction.

A practitioner would say: "Correct exposure and white balance on each shot, match the sunset shots to the noon ones, then apply one warm grade to the whole video."

**Assumptions to confirm.**

1. Do you want it to look (a) natural, or (b) stylised? Recommended: (a). Changes: how far the grade may push colour.

Offer: three short descriptions of looks (true-to-life, warm film, cool and muted) so the user can pick one instead of describing it.

### Starting a PhD

**Picture.** Goal: become a researcher. Plan: "apply to the best-ranked programmes I can get into". Known: research interest. Constraints: savings for one year.

**Gap that matters most.** Day to day, a PhD is shaped by the advisor more than the programme's rank, and practitioners advise talking to an advisor's current students before accepting. A rank-only plan never looks at the advisor.

**Other gaps.**

1. In many fields most graduates do not end up in permanent academic posts. Changes: whether "researcher" means academia, which decides how much the programme's industry links matter. Evidence: the field's own career surveys. Next move: look up your field's figures.
2. "Savings for one year" against a programme of several years. Funded positions with a stipend are the norm in some fields and rare in others. Next move: check funding terms before applying, not after an offer.

**Assumptions to confirm.**

1. Does "researcher" mean (a) academia only, or (b) academia or industry? Recommended: (b). Changes: which programmes and advisors fit.
2. Would you accept (a) only funded places, or (b) self-funded too? Recommended: (a), given one year of savings.

### Nothing to find

**Picture.** Goal: a laptop for programming. Plan: three named models compared on RAM, battery, keyboard, and repair record, with a budget and a return window.

**Finding.** The picture already covers what a reviewer would check, including the return window that guards against the rest. No gap passes the tests. Say so, name the references used, and stop.
